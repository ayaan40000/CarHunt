Gov & Public Auctions — Texas

CarHunt is not Facebook-only. Texas governments and public bodies sell their used vehicles to the public — fixed-price at a state storefront, and by online auction. This page is the source directory and the rules for how those cars join the board. A second, clearly separated section below covers bank & credit-union repossessed cars — the other below-market pool the public can actually buy.

Does Texas sell used government cars? Yes — two ways

1. Fixed price, no bidding. The Texas Facilities Commission (TFC) runs the State Surplus store in Austin. Vehicles sit on the lot with the price and mileage on the windshield. You can test-drive on the lot and bring your own mechanic. Pay the windshield price — cash or a bank cashier's check — and the title is mailed to you in about 21 days.

2. Online auctions. State agencies, cities, counties, school districts, universities and the federal government sell surplus vehicles through a handful of auction portals (below). Everything is sold as-is, no warranty, usually with a buyer's premium on top of the winning bid.

Scale: aggregators counted roughly 1,100 government car/SUV auctions live in Texas in November 2026, and the median winning bid across Texas government car/SUV sales last year was about $5,476 — well inside the $15,000 budget. The catch is not price. It is mileage and spec, covered below.

GovDeals

Online auctionCities · counties · schools · universities

The largest government-surplus marketplace in the US. Texas sellers include the City of Sugar Land, Texas A&M and Texas Tech, school districts, counties and the State Comptroller — hundreds of Texas agencies, with new vehicle listings every week.

  • Buyer's premium: set by each seller — 5%, 7.5%, 10% or 12.5% on top of the bid. The grade math must use bid + premium.
  • First-time buyers: purchases are capped at $1,000 during a 30+ day probation period. Register early, before a car worth chasing appears.

What fits CarHunt — and what does not

Fits Agency fleet cars and SUVs at or under 100,000 miles — typically admin and pool vehicles (sedans like Malibu, Fusion, Sonata; small SUVs). Clean government titles, VIN and mileage published in the listing.

Usually fails Police packages (Tahoe PPV, Explorer Interceptor): almost always 140,000+ miles and no consumer infotainment, so no factory Android Auto. Trucks: excluded body type, and they dominate surplus volume.

Excluded by default Impound, abandoned and seized vehicles (city auto pounds): unknown history, many do not run, some must be towed out by a licensed wrecker. They stay off the board unless one specific car passes the normal verification.

  • All-in price: grades use bid + buyer's premium + stated fees, never the bare bid.
  • As-is means as-is: no warranty, preview/inspect before bidding where offered, strict pickup deadlines, storage fees after.
  • A live bid is not a price: a running auction's current bid proves nothing. Only a fixed storefront price, or a closed/verified result, can be graded.

How these cars join the board

  • Records from these portals carry listing_source naming the portal (GovDeals, Public Surplus (TX), Rene Bates, Lone Star, TFC Surplus, GSA) and seller_type "government" — the same schema and grading rubric as every other card.
  • Screening follows the Carvana rule in docs/METHOD.md: the listing itself must show an eligible family, a price under $15,000 and mileage at or under 100,000. Auction finds are staged as sourced-unverified with the auction end date until verified; nothing ships with an unconfirmed must-have.
  • The reliability cross-check and exclusion list apply unchanged — a government seller does not rescue an excluded model.

Bank & credit-union repos

Are repos cheaper? Usually, yes — with a condition attached. When a borrower stops paying, the lender takes the car back and wants it gone: it is not a car dealer, it earns nothing by holding stock, and every week the car sits costs it money. Direct repo prices are therefore set against wholesale/book values and the unpaid loan balance, not dealer retail. Independent buyer guides put typical direct-sale repo pricing roughly 10–30% below retail guide value, and the worked examples line up — one current lender listing carried on RepoFinder shows a JD Power retail value of $24,125 against a $21,500 asking price. The condition: the car is sold as-is, no warranty, no reconditioning, and how a defaulting owner maintained it is a gamble. CarHunt does not take the discount on faith — every repo is graded against the same market estimates as every other card, so a discount has to be real to score.

Where most repos actually go: the big lenders send theirs to dealer-only wholesale auctions (named below). The public pool is mostly credit unions and smaller banks selling their own repos directly — a handful of cars per institution at a time, which is why the aggregator below matters.

Not buyable without a dealer license

  • Manheim, ADESA, SmartAuction — the wholesale auctions where most bank repos, including nearly all big-lender volume, are actually sold. Licensed dealers only. A repo that goes through Manheim reaches the public later, as a dealer's retail listing at a retail price — the discount is gone by then.
  • Copart (Texas) — public members exist, but under Copart's own Texas licensing table a private buyer may bid directly only on salvage-title lots and lots flagged "No License Required"; a clean-title car must go through a registered broker, with the broker's fee on top. CarHunt requires a clean title, so Copart is out — and its stock is mostly insurance salvage anyway. Licensing table summary: Copart license requirements by state.
  • IAA / IAAI (Texas) — public buyers can register ($225/yr, government photo ID, per IAA's own help center), but Texas public buyers bid on salvage titles only (in-state buyers limited to 5 a year); clear-title cars need a broker. Same conclusion as Copart.

Repo price, condition and title — the honest picture

  • Price: asking prices and offers, not auctions, on the direct channels — and no buyer's premium, which is the quiet advantage over the gov auction portals. Negotiation is normal: some lenders price with "OBO", and all of them would rather move a car than store it.
  • Condition: strictly as-is everywhere. No reconditioning; inspection ranges from an appointment viewing and test drive (Rave) to bid-first formats with no test drive. A repo can be a well-kept car whose owner lost a job, or a neglected one — lender listings range from "excellent shape" to honest defect lists (one current RepoFinder listing discloses brakes, exhaust, rust holes and engine codes). Inspection before money is the whole game.
  • Title: the strong point. The seller is the lienholder; on payoff it releases its lien and transfers a title that is normally clean — Rave states this as an express warranty of ownership. A branded title is still possible on any individual car and still excludes it here; title status is verified per car, never assumed.
  • Cash: direct repo sales are cash-friendly — cashier's check or wire within days of acceptance is the standard term (three business days at Rave). The financing the credit unions advertise on these pages is irrelevant to CarHunt's cash frame.
  • Fit: repo stock is consumer cars, not fleet trucks — the target sedan and SUV families show up regularly, and a good share of the listings sit under 100,000 miles, where 2017+ model years mostly carry factory Android Auto per the matrix. Volume per lender is tiny; the pool only works in aggregate.

How repo cars join the board

  • Records carry listing_source naming the channel (RepoFinder, Rave Financial, RBFCU) and seller_type "lender" — same schema, same grading rubric.
  • Only direct public sales qualify: a fixed asking price, or a published offer/bid format open to anyone without a dealer license. A live bid is not a price (same rule as the gov auctions) — bid-format cars are staged, not graded, until a real asking figure exists.
  • Every repo card is flagged sold as-is in its notes, with condition unknowns listed as verification blockers. The exclusion list, reliability cross-check and title rules apply unchanged.
