FAQ & Help

Which cars should I pursue?

  • 2019 Ford Fusion Hybrid SE — first choice. Proven hybrid system, AA standard, safety kit standard. Regular Hybrid only — never the Energi plug-in.
  • 2017–18 Fusion Hybrid SE / Fusion 2.5L SE with the 8-inch SYNC 3 screen — same idea, older. Small 4.2-inch screen = no AA, ever.
  • 2016–17 Honda Accord EX/EX-L, 2.4L and Honda Civic Sedan 2.0L — 2016–18 EX and above; 2019 Sport (the only 2019 2.0L with Android Auto) — avoid 2019 Civic EX and above — that is the 1.5T. Test the Civic’s A/C hard; Honda extended the condenser warranty on some 2016–17 Civics.
  • 2019 Kia Forte (any trim) and 2018 Hyundai Elantra SEL — value picks. A 2019 Elantra needs proof the piston-ring recall (21V-301) was done.
  • 2018–19 Subaru Legacy Premium, late-2018/2019 Mazda6 Touring (non-turbo), 2017–19 Chevy Impala LT V6, 2020 Toyota Camry — alternatives, each with one named check in the Research doc.

Which should I avoid?

  • Ford Fusion 1.5L EcoBoost — coolant can leak into the cylinders; engine replacement can cost $3,500–$8,000.
  • Honda 1.5T (Accord/Civic 2016–20) — oil dilution: fuel thins the oil, especially on short trips.
  • Hyundai Sonata / Kia Optima 2.4L & 2.0T “Theta II” — connecting-rod bearing failure, knock, seizure, fire risk. Any knock or patchy oil history: walk.
  • Nissan Altima 2013–18, Ford Focus/Fiesta automatics — CVT / PowerShift dual-clutch failures. Fusion never had PowerShift — do not confuse them.
  • Plug-in hybrids in this budget (Fusion Energi, Sonata Plug-in) — aged big batteries and a Fusion Energi fire recall (do-not-charge).
  • Any rebuilt / salvage / flood title, any seller who refuses an inspection — in Houston, that rule alone saves most bad buys.

Jargon in one line: Trim = version of the same car (LX basic, EX mid, Touring high). CVT = automatic with a belt instead of fixed gears. Hybrid eCVT is a different, simpler, stronger device — not a belt CVT. PPI = pre-purchase inspection by your own mechanic.

More jargon: KSDS = Hyundai/Kia knock-sensor software that detects engine knock and limps the car — a warning system, not a repair. UVO = Kia's infotainment system. MyLink = Chevrolet's infotainment system. Display Audio = Honda's touchscreen system.

Check a VIN — in this order

  1. NHTSA VIN Decoder (free) — proves the VIN matches the year/make/model/engine in the ad. Proves nothing about condition or history.
  2. NHTSA Recalls (free) — proves which safety recalls are still open on that exact car. Does not show completed recalls — get a dealer printout for those.
  3. NICB VINCheck (free, 5/day) — proves an insurer reported it stolen or totalled. A clean result does NOT guarantee a clean car.
  4. NMVTIS via TxDMV Title Check (~$10–20) — title brands from any state (Salvage, Rebuilt, Flood, odometer brands). This is the title-washing detector. Start at txdmv.gov, search “Title Check.”
  5. Carfax (~$45–50) or AutoCheck (~$25–30) — paid history for a finalist only. Carfax is stronger on service detail; AutoCheck on auctions. Neither proves anything that was never reported — believe a bad inspection over a clean report.
  6. In person (free, non-negotiable) — VIN plate (windshield) = door sticker = title, and the seller’s photo ID name = name on the title. Any mismatch: stop.

Pre-purchase inspection checklist ($100–$150, your mechanic, not the seller’s)

  • Full scan of all modules; hybrid battery cell voltages on hybrids; watch for cleared codes (incomplete readiness monitors).
  • Cold start observed — never accept a pre-warmed car. Listen for knock/rattle; watch smoke.
  • Oil & coolant check: over-full oil smelling of fuel (dilution), glitter/metal in oil (bearing wear), coolant pressure test.
  • On a lift: crash/flood signs, frame rails, paint thickness, suspension, leaks, tyre dates — all four tyres matching on AWD cars.
  • Houston flood protocol, every car: silt under carpets / spare-tyre well, rusty seat rails and under-dash metal, musty smell, fogged lamps.
  • A/C torture test: max cold 10 minutes, vent temperature measured — critical on Civics.
  • Road test: CVT judder on gentle take-off, low-speed shudder, highway merge, straight braking, whine with radio off.
  • Your phone + cable: Android Auto launch, reverse camera, every screen function (watch a parked Mazda screen 60 seconds for “ghost touch”).
  • Written list of what is due now — add it to the price before negotiating. Walk away for: frame repair, flood evidence, metal in fluids, bad hybrid cell spread, title/odometer mismatch.

Marketplace scam red flags

  • Curbstoner: “private seller” with many cars sold, meets in a parking lot, cannot say how long they owned it.
  • Title jumping: title in a stranger’s name, blank buyer line, “saves you tax.” ID must match title — no match, no money.
  • Washed salvage: 25–40% under market, fresh out-of-state title, “clean blue title!” — run NICB + NMVTIS before travelling. Note: in Texas a clean title is itself blue — what matters is the brand printed on the title (e.g. “Rebuilt Salvage”), not the colour, so verify the brand on the physical title.
  • Bait listing: stock photos, no Texas plate, pushed off-platform, deployment/divorce/shipping story.
  • Deposit scam: never send Zelle/Cash App/Venmo to hold a car you have not stood next to.
  • Odometer games: worn pedals/seats at “low” miles, readings going backwards in the history report.
  • “Just needs a sensor / a recharge”: price it as the full repair until your inspection proves otherwise.
  • Meet in daylight, public/camera-covered place, bring a second person, finish money + title at a bank or county tax office.

Car-buying paperwork in Texas — private-party, cash

The exact steps for buying from a private seller in Texas and getting the title into your name. Fees change — confirm current amounts at txdmv.gov or the Harris County Tax Assessor-Collector before you go.

1. Before any money moves

  • The seller must be the owner named on the front of the title. If the title is in someone else's name (an “open title”), walk away — title jumping is illegal in Texas and the county can refuse the transfer.
  • Match the VIN in three places: dashboard plate, driver's door sticker, and the title. Any mismatch: stop.
  • Read the brand printed on the title itself (“Rebuilt Salvage”, “Flood”, etc.). A blue Texas title is not automatically clean — the printed brand is what counts, not the colour.
  • Lien printed on the title? The seller must hand over the original lien release. Without it the title cannot transfer cleanly.
  • Run the VIN first: NICB VINCheck (theft / total loss, free), NHTSA recalls (free), and an NMVTIS title check via TxDMV “Title Check” (~$10–20).

2. Paperwork at the sale — one sitting, ideally at a bank

  • Certificate of Title: the seller signs the back, prints their name exactly as it appears on the front, dates it, and fills in the odometer reading. You sign as purchaser. Never accept a title with the buyer line left blank for someone else to fill in later.
  • Form 130-U (Application for Texas Title and/or Registration): the seller signs this too — it is the form the county actually processes.
  • Odometer disclosure is part of the title transfer for vehicles 20 model years old or newer.
  • Bill of sale: not required by the state, but get one anyway — date, price, VIN, both names and signatures. It is your proof if anything is disputed later.
  • Plates stay with the seller in Texas (they can move them to their next car). You get new plates at registration. Need to drive it first? TxDMV issues a free 5-day Vehicle Transit Permit online.

3. Within 30 days — title it in your name

  • File at your county tax assessor-collector office (Harris County for most Houston buyers) within 30 calendar days of the purchase date. Late filing adds a penalty of 5% of the tax, and another 5% after 30 more days.
  • Bring: the signed title, the signed Form 130-U, photo ID, proof of insurance in your name, and payment. Insurance comes first — the county must see it.
  • Sales tax: 6.25% of the greater of the sale price or the Standard Presumptive Value (SPV, TxDMV's book value for the car). Paid a genuine bargain well under SPV? A certified appraisal (Form 14-128) filed within the deadline can base the tax on the appraised value instead.
  • Fees: title transfer fee ($28 or $33 depending on county), plus registration and plates (base registration about $50.75 plus county fees).
  • Emissions: Harris County requires an emissions test. (The statewide safety inspection was dropped in 2025; emissions testing remains in metro counties.)

4. After the transfer

  • The seller should file a Vehicle Transfer Notification (Form VTR-214, free online) within 30 days — it protects them from tolls and tickets after the sale, and it cleanly ends the car's link to them. Ask them to do it while you are together.
  • Keep copies of everything: the title (photograph front and back before filing), Form 130-U, the bill of sale, any lien release, and your insurance card.
  • Lose the signed title before filing and you are into the bonded-title process — slow and paid. Photograph it the day you buy.

Walk-away paperwork red flags

  • Title in someone else's name, or a seller who “will sign it later”.
  • No lien release for a lien printed on the title.
  • Seller refuses to meet at a bank or the county office, or pressures you to skip the 130-U.
  • Price far below SPV with no story that checks out — the tax office will use SPV anyway.
